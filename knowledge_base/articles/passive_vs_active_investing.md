# Passive vs. Active Investing

## The Core Question
Can professional fund managers consistently beat the market after fees? Decades of research provide a clear answer: most cannot.

## Active Investing
Active managers research individual securities, make buy/sell decisions, and try to outperform a benchmark index.

**Cost**: Typically 0.5–1.5% expense ratio + trading costs.
**Goal**: Beat the benchmark.
**Claim**: Skilled analysis identifies mispriced securities.

## Passive Investing
Passive funds track an index, holding all its constituents at market weights. No security selection.

**Cost**: Typically 0.03–0.20% expense ratio.
**Goal**: Match the benchmark (minus tiny fees).
**Logic**: If you can't beat the market, stop trying to.

## The Evidence Against Active Management

### SPIVA Report (S&P)
Over 15 years ending 2023:
- ~90% of US large-cap active funds underperformed the S&P 500.
- ~85% of mid-cap funds underperformed.
- International funds: similar results.

### Persistence Problem
Funds that beat the market in one decade rarely repeat in the next. Last decade's winners are no better than random at predicting next decade's winners.

### The Arithmetic
William Sharpe's proof: Before fees, the average active investor must exactly equal market return (passive investors hold the market; active investors collectively also hold the market). After fees, the average active investor underperforms by exactly average fees. A logical, mathematical certainty — not an empirical observation.

## When Active Management Might Add Value
Active management has plausibly outperformed in:
- **Less efficient markets**: Small-cap stocks, emerging markets, high-yield bonds — where information is less available and prices less perfectly set.
- **Alternative assets**: Private equity, venture capital, real estate — where index funds don't exist.
- **Specific managers**: A tiny minority (< 5%) of managers have shown persistent skill — but identifying them in advance is extremely difficult.

## Factor Investing: Middle Ground
A hybrid approach: passive but tilting toward factors with historical risk premia (value, small-cap, momentum, quality, low volatility).

- Systematic and rules-based like passive.
- Captures documented return premiums like active.
- Lower cost than active (0.10–0.30%).

## The Fee Impact Over Time
$100,000 invested for 30 years at 7% gross:
- 0.05% passive: ~$755,000
- 1.00% active: ~$574,000
- 1.50% active: ~$516,000

The active fund must outperform by its fee just to break even. Most don't.

## Practical Conclusion
For most individual investors:
1. Use low-cost index funds as the core (80-100% of portfolio).
2. If you want to try active, limit to 10-20% and track performance honestly vs. a benchmark.
3. No one is obligated to be passive — but the burden of proof is on active strategies to justify higher costs.
