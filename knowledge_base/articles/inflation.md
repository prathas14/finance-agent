# Inflation

## What Is Inflation?
Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services rises over time, eroding purchasing power. If inflation is 3%, something that costs $100 today will cost $103 next year.

## How Inflation Is Measured
- **CPI (Consumer Price Index)**: Tracks price changes in a basket of common goods and services. The most widely used inflation measure.
- **PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures)**: The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation measure. Broader than CPI.
- **Core Inflation**: CPI or PCE excluding food and energy (more volatile categories).
- **PPI (Producer Price Index)**: Measures inflation at the wholesale/producer level. Often a leading indicator of CPI.

## Causes of Inflation
- **Demand-pull**: Too much money chasing too few goods (economy overheating).
- **Cost-push**: Rising production costs (e.g. oil prices, wages) passed on to consumers.
- **Monetary**: Excess money supply growth (printing money).

## Effects on Investments
| Asset | Inflation Impact |
|-------|-----------------|
| Cash/Savings | Purchasing power erodes |
| Bonds (fixed rate) | Real return falls; prices drop |
| Stocks | Mixed — companies can raise prices, but costs rise too |
| Real Estate | Generally appreciates with inflation |
| TIPS | Protected — principal adjusts with CPI |
| Gold/Commodities | Often rise with inflation |

## The Fed and Inflation
The Federal Reserve targets 2% annual inflation. When inflation runs too high, the Fed raises interest rates to slow borrowing and spending. When too low or deflation threatens, it cuts rates.

## Real vs. Nominal Returns
- **Nominal return**: The stated return on an investment.
- **Real return**: Nominal return minus inflation.

If your portfolio earns 8% and inflation is 3%, your real return is ~5%.

## Rule of 70
Divide 70 by the inflation rate to estimate how many years it takes for prices to double.
At 3.5% inflation: 70 / 3.5 = 20 years to double prices.
