# IRA: Traditional and Roth

## What Is an IRA?
An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is a personal retirement savings account with tax advantages. Unlike 401(k)s, IRAs are not employer-sponsored — anyone with earned income can open one at a brokerage.

## Contribution Limits (2024)
- Annual limit: $7,000/year
- Catch-up (age 50+): Additional $1,000/year ($8,000 total)
- Combined limit across all IRAs (Traditional + Roth): $7,000/$8,000 total

## Traditional IRA

### Tax Treatment
- Contributions may be **tax-deductible** depending on income and whether you have a workplace plan.
- Growth is tax-deferred.
- Withdrawals in retirement taxed as ordinary income.

### Deductibility Rules (2024, single filer with workplace plan)
- Income < $77,000: Fully deductible
- $77,000–$87,000: Partially deductible
- > $87,000: Not deductible (but can still contribute — "non-deductible IRA")

### Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)
Must start withdrawing at age 73. Annual RMD amount based on account balance and IRS life expectancy tables.

## Roth IRA

### Tax Treatment
- Contributions made with **after-tax dollars** — no deduction now.
- Growth is completely **tax-free**.
- Qualified withdrawals in retirement are **tax-free** (including gains).

### Income Limits (2024, single filer)
- Income < $146,000: Full contribution allowed
- $146,000–$161,000: Partial contribution
- > $161,000: Cannot contribute directly (see Backdoor Roth)

### Advantages Over Traditional IRA
- No RMDs during your lifetime.
- Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn any time, tax and penalty-free.
- Better for people who expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement.

## Backdoor Roth IRA
High earners above the Roth income limit can:
1. Contribute to a non-deductible Traditional IRA.
2. Immediately convert it to a Roth IRA.
This is legal and widely used. Watch out for the "pro-rata rule" if you have other Traditional IRA funds.

## Roth Conversion
Converting Traditional IRA funds to Roth. You pay income tax on the converted amount in the year of conversion. Makes sense when:
- You expect higher taxes in the future.
- You're in a temporarily low-income year.
- You want to leave tax-free assets to heirs.

## Self-Employed Retirement Accounts
- **SEP-IRA**: Contribute up to 25% of net self-employment income, max $69,000 (2024). Simple to set up.
- **SIMPLE IRA**: For small businesses. Employee contributes up to $16,000 (2024), employer must match.
- **Solo 401(k)**: For self-employed with no employees. Can contribute as both employer and employee — highest limits.

## IRA vs. 401(k) — Which First?
1. Contribute to 401(k) up to employer match (free money).
2. Max out Roth IRA ($7,000).
3. Go back and max out 401(k) ($23,000).
4. Then taxable brokerage account.
