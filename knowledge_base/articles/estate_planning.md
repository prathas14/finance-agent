# Estate Planning Basics

## What Is Estate Planning?
Estate planning is the process of arranging what happens to your assets, your dependents, and your healthcare decisions when you die or become incapacitated. It's not just for the wealthy — everyone with dependents, assets, or strong preferences should have a basic plan.

## Why People Avoid It (And Why They Shouldn't)
Discomfort with mortality causes most people to procrastinate. Without an estate plan:
- State law ("intestacy") decides who gets your assets — may not be what you wanted.
- A court appoints a guardian for your children — may not be who you'd choose.
- Your healthcare wishes may not be followed.
- Your family faces expensive, time-consuming probate court proceedings.

## Core Documents

### Will (Last Will and Testament)
Documents who receives your assets, names an executor (person who carries out the will), and names a guardian for minor children. Goes through probate (public court process). Does NOT override beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance.

### Revocable Living Trust
Assets placed in the trust avoid probate. More privacy and control than a will. More expensive to set up but saves costs and time at death. Allows you to control distribution terms (e.g. children receive funds at 25, not 18). Becomes essential for blended families, complex assets, or privacy concerns.

### Durable Power of Attorney (POA)
Names someone to make financial decisions on your behalf if you become incapacitated. "Durable" means it remains valid even if you lose mental capacity.

### Healthcare Proxy / Medical Power of Attorney
Names someone to make medical decisions if you cannot.

### Advance Healthcare Directive / Living Will
Specifies your wishes for medical treatment in end-of-life situations — ventilators, feeding tubes, etc. Removes burden from family and ensures your wishes are followed.

## Beneficiary Designations
Retirement accounts (401k, IRA), life insurance, and payable-on-death (POD) accounts pass directly to named beneficiaries — completely bypassing your will and probate.

Keep these updated. Common mistake: leaving an ex-spouse as beneficiary after divorce.

Priority: beneficiary designation > trust > will.

## Estate Taxes
- **Federal estate tax**: Only applies to estates over $13.61 million per person (2024). Affects very few.
- **State estate taxes**: Many states have lower thresholds. Check your state's laws.
- **Step-up in basis**: Inherited assets receive a stepped-up cost basis — eliminates capital gains on appreciation during decedent's lifetime.
- **Gift tax exclusion**: You can give $18,000/year to any individual (2024) without gift tax implications.

## Basic Estate Plan for Most People
1. Write a will — name executor, beneficiaries, guardian for children.
2. Set beneficiary designations on all financial accounts and insurance.
3. Create a durable power of attorney.
4. Create a healthcare proxy and advance directive.
5. Consider a revocable living trust if your estate is complex or you value privacy.
6. Review and update every 3-5 years or after major life events (marriage, divorce, birth, death, major asset change).
