# Bond Yield and Duration

## Understanding Yield

### Current Yield
Annual coupon payment / Current market price.
A $1,000 bond paying $50/year trading at $950 has a current yield of 5.26%.

### Yield to Maturity (YTM)
The total return expected if a bond is held until maturity, accounting for:
- Coupon payments
- Difference between purchase price and face value

YTM is the most important yield measure. It's the IRR of all future bond cash flows.

### Yield to Call (YTC)
For callable bonds (which the issuer can repay early), the yield assuming the bond is called at the earliest call date.

## The Yield Curve
The yield curve plots yields for bonds of the same credit quality but different maturities.

### Normal (Upward-Sloping) Yield Curve
Short-term yields < long-term yields. Normal state — investors demand more for locking up money longer.

### Inverted Yield Curve
Short-term yields > long-term yields. Historically a reliable recession predictor. Occurred before every US recession since 1950.

### Flat Yield Curve
Short and long yields are similar. Signals economic uncertainty.

## Duration

### Macaulay Duration
The weighted average time to receive all bond cash flows. Measured in years. A bond with Macaulay duration of 7 years will recoup its investment in 7 years on average.

### Modified Duration
Estimates the price sensitivity of a bond to interest rate changes.

Modified Duration = Macaulay Duration / (1 + YTM)

Rule of thumb: A bond with modified duration of 5 will fall ~5% in price if interest rates rise 1%.

### Key Duration Insights
- Longer maturity = higher duration = more interest rate sensitivity.
- Zero-coupon bonds have duration equal to their maturity (highest sensitivity).
- High-coupon bonds have lower duration than low-coupon bonds of the same maturity.

## Price vs. Yield Relationship
Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions:
- Interest rates rise → bond prices fall
- Interest rates fall → bond prices rise

This is the most important relationship in fixed income investing.

Example: You own a $1,000 bond paying 3%. New bonds are issued at 5%. Your bond becomes less attractive, so its price falls until its yield equals ~5%.

## Convexity
Duration is a linear approximation. Convexity accounts for the curved (non-linear) relationship between price and yield. Bonds with higher convexity are more valuable because they gain more when rates fall and lose less when rates rise.
