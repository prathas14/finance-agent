# Portfolio Rebalancing

## What Is Rebalancing?
Rebalancing is the process of realigning your portfolio's asset weights back to your target allocation. Over time, assets that perform well grow to a larger share than intended, increasing your risk profile beyond what you chose.

## Why Rebalance?
Example: You start with 80% stocks / 20% bonds. After 3 years of strong stock performance, your portfolio drifts to 92% stocks / 8% bonds. You're now taking significantly more risk than intended — and at a time when stocks may be expensive.

Rebalancing restores your risk level and forces a "buy low, sell high" discipline — selling appreciated assets and buying laggards.

## Rebalancing Methods

### Calendar Rebalancing
Rebalance on a set schedule (annually, semi-annually, quarterly). Simplest approach. Annual rebalancing is most common and sufficient for most investors.

### Threshold (Band) Rebalancing
Rebalance when an asset class drifts beyond a set percentage (e.g. 5% absolute or 25% relative from target).

Example: 5% absolute threshold. Target is 60% stocks. Rebalance only when stocks reach 65% or fall to 55%.

Pros: Only trades when meaningful drift occurs. Less frequent trading.

### Hybrid: Calendar + Threshold
Check annually. Only rebalance if drift exceeds threshold. Best of both worlds — disciplined but not over-trading.

## Cash Flow Rebalancing
Instead of selling appreciated assets (which triggers taxes), direct new contributions and dividends to underweight asset classes. The most tax-efficient rebalancing method.

Particularly effective during the accumulation phase when you're regularly adding money.

## Tax Considerations

### In Tax-Advantaged Accounts (IRA, 401k)
Rebalance freely — no tax consequences. This is the ideal place for rebalancing trades.

### In Taxable Accounts
Selling appreciated assets triggers capital gains taxes. Strategies to minimize:
- Use cash flow rebalancing first.
- Rebalance via tax-advantaged accounts only.
- Wait for positions to qualify for long-term rates (>1 year).
- Harvest losses to offset gains when rebalancing.

## Does Rebalancing Improve Returns?
Evidence is mixed:
- In trending markets (US stocks 2010s): Not rebalancing would have given higher returns.
- In volatile/reverting markets: Rebalancing helps.
- The main benefit is **risk control**, not return enhancement.

Studies suggest annual or threshold rebalancing adds 0.1–0.5% annually in risk-adjusted returns — modest but meaningful over decades.

## Rebalancing with Target-Date Funds
Target-date funds rebalance automatically. Investors in these funds don't need to rebalance manually — it's built in. This is a key advantage of all-in-one fund solutions.

## Common Rebalancing Mistakes
- Rebalancing too frequently (quarterly or monthly) increases costs and taxes.
- Ignoring taxes when rebalancing in taxable accounts.
- Not considering all accounts together (treat all accounts as one portfolio).
- Over-reaction to normal market fluctuations.
