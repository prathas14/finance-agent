# International Investing

## Why Invest Internationally?
The US represents ~60% of global stock market capitalisation but only ~4% of the world's population and ~24% of global GDP. Investing internationally provides:

- **Broader diversification**: Access to companies and sectors not available in the US.
- **Valuation opportunity**: International markets often trade at lower valuations than the US.
- **Currency diversification**: Exposure to multiple currencies.
- **Growth exposure**: Emerging markets offer higher growth potential (with higher risk).

## US-Only vs. Global Portfolio
The US stock market has outperformed international markets significantly in the 2010s, leading many investors to question international diversification. However:
- Historical precedent: Japan, then Germany, then the US dominated at different times. Dominance shifts.
- Valuations: US stocks have traded at large premium to international (higher P/E). International may mean-revert.
- Periods of international outperformance do occur (early 2000s, 1970s).

**Vanguard recommendation**: 40% of equity in international stocks.
**Many US investors hold**: 20-30% international.
**Home bias is real and usually excessive** — most non-US investors should hold more US exposure; most US investors hold too little international.

## Developed vs. Emerging Markets

### Developed Markets
Mature economies with stable institutions, rule of law, and deep capital markets.
- **EAFE**: Europe, Australasia, Far East (Japan, UK, France, Germany, Switzerland, etc.)
- VEA, EFA: Major developed market ex-US ETFs.

### Emerging Markets
Faster growing economies with higher political, currency, and regulatory risk.
- **Major markets**: China, India, Brazil, Taiwan, South Korea, South Africa.
- VWO, EEM: Major emerging market ETFs.
- Higher expected return + higher volatility than developed.

### Frontier Markets
Pre-emerging markets. Very illiquid, high risk, small allocations only. Vietnam, Nigeria, Kazakhstan.

## Currency Risk
International investments involve currency risk — the investment's home currency may depreciate against the USD.

- **Foreign currency falling vs. USD**: Return is reduced for US investors.
- **Foreign currency rising vs. USD**: Return is enhanced for US investors.

Currency hedged ETFs (HEFA, HEDJ) eliminate this but have higher expense ratios and miss currency upside.

Over long periods, currency effects tend to average out. Most long-term investors accept unhedged currency exposure.

## Political and Regulatory Risk
International investing — especially in emerging markets — involves:
- **Political risk**: Government policy changes, nationalisation.
- **Regulatory risk**: Sudden rule changes affecting foreign investors (China crackdowns, Russia sanctions).
- **Corporate governance**: Weaker shareholder protections in many countries.

Diversification across many countries reduces but doesn't eliminate this risk.

## Practical Implementation
Simple international exposure:
- **VXUS** (Vanguard Total International Stock): ~7,800 stocks in 47 countries. One fund for all international exposure.
- **VEA** (developed markets only) + **VWO** (emerging markets): More control.
- **VT** (Vanguard Total World Stock): Holds both US and international in one fund. Global market cap weights (~60% US / 40% international).
