# Behavioral Finance

## What Is Behavioral Finance?
Behavioral finance studies how psychological biases and emotional factors cause investors to make irrational decisions, leading to predictable — and costly — departures from rational investing.

## Why It Matters
The average investor significantly underperforms the market not because of fees, but because of behavior. DALBAR's annual studies consistently show individual investors earn 2–3% less than the funds they invest in — because they buy high and sell low.

## Key Cognitive Biases

### Loss Aversion
Losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good. A $1,000 loss hurts more than a $1,000 gain pleases. This leads to:
- Holding losing positions too long (refusing to realize loss).
- Selling winners too quickly (locking in gain).
- Avoiding stocks altogether after a market crash.

### Overconfidence
Investors consistently overestimate their ability to pick stocks or time the market. Studies show most individual stock pickers underperform simple index funds.

### Anchoring
Over-relying on the first piece of information seen. "This stock was $100; at $60 it's a bargain." The past price is irrelevant to future value.

### Confirmation Bias
Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs, ignoring contradicting evidence. Leads to concentrated bets on "sure things."

### Herd Mentality
Following the crowd. Buying during bubbles because "everyone is doing it." Selling during crashes because "everyone is panicking." This is how individual investors reliably buy high and sell low.

### Recency Bias
Overweighting recent events. After 3 years of bull market: "stocks always go up." After 3 months of bear market: "stocks are too risky."

### Mental Accounting
Treating money differently based on its source or intended use. "Lottery winnings" feel free to gamble with. This creates irrational inconsistencies.

### Availability Heuristic
Overweighting easily recalled events (vivid, recent, emotional). After a plane crash: overestimate flight risk. After a publicized stock fraud: avoid all stocks.

### Endowment Effect
Valuing something you own more than an identical thing you don't. Reluctant to sell inherited stocks at a loss even though cost basis is irrelevant to future performance.

## Emotional Investing Traps

### The Investor Emotion Cycle
Markets move in cycles; investor emotions follow in the wrong direction:
- **Optimism → Excitement → Thrill → Euphoria** (peak confidence, maximum investment) → market top
- **Anxiety → Denial → Fear → Desperation → Panic → Capitulation** → market bottom
- **Depression → Hope → Relief** → recovery and the cycle repeats

The average investor is maximally invested at the top and sells at the bottom.

### FOMO (Fear of Missing Out)
Buying assets because others are getting rich. Classic driver of bubbles.

### Panic Selling
Selling everything during a crash. Permanently locks in losses and misses recovery. The investor who sold in March 2020 and waited missed a 100% recovery in 18 months.

## How to Overcome Biases
1. **Automate investments**: Dollar-cost averaging removes emotional timing decisions.
2. **Write an investment policy statement**: Define your strategy in writing before markets move.
3. **Don't check your portfolio daily**: Reduces emotional reactions to short-term noise.
4. **Rebalance mechanically**: Rules-based selling and buying overrides emotion.
5. **Think in probabilities**: Bad outcomes don't mean bad decisions.
6. **Have an investment checklist**: Pre-commit to rational criteria.
7. **Work with a fee-only advisor**: Accountability partner for emotional decisions.
